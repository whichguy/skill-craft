ShipLoop · the spec stage · 20 and 21 November 2026

Mark the open points. Decide each one once.

You asked for a prompt that turns a one-sentence request into a full specification. Two days of experiments here, and results from two neighbouring sessions, changed our picture of what the specification stage needs. This page walks through what we learned, in the order we learned it.

Sources: docs/spec-elaboration-journal.md (sections cited in each part) and the Ratchet session's docs/ratchet-pilot-journal.md, both on main. Hover or tab to any mark for its exact value.

In one minute

  • More elaboration did not hold up. On a test bench, longer and more searching spec prompts covered more of a hidden checklist. The gains were mostly points the released text already touched, plus limits nobody asked for. ShipLoop's prompt was not changed.
  • The bench and the real runs differ in three ways. Bench specs were many times longer (11 to 17 times on Sol). But the bench gave one sentence where real cases give a page, had no repository, and its sessions read ShipLoop's reference guides, which real builders almost never open. Real deliveries also showed a gap of their own: on Sol, builders pick limits and never write them down.
  • Leaving a point open beat deciding it. In the Ratchet session, a text that marks undecided points Open: wrote a quarter of the clauses, fewer of them failed on a correct product, and its first builds of the due-times case passed all 8 held-out checks where texts that decide had passed 2 or 3.
  • The answers then belong in the plan. Tests written from the plan passed on builds they had never seen. On a correct product that chose different answers, they lost 10 to 12 items.
  • A ShipLoop text fix qualified. The fix released as ShipLoop 2.4.1 passed checks that were written down before any result was read.

Journal sections 1 to 28 · 20 and 21 November

A test bench for the spec stage on its own

Each prompt text was given four one-sentence requests and wrote the specification alone, with no repository and nothing after it: grokbot-salesforce (your Grok bot in a Salesforce org), gym-waitlist, invoice-reminders and csv-user-import. Before any run this session wrote a hidden checklist of 89 points a good specification should cover, in ten categories (edge cases, unhappy paths, data, permissions, operators and more). Two judge models, Opus and Astra, marked every specification against it. The drivers were the ones you named: Sonnet at high effort and GPT 6.2 Sol at medium.

  1. Round 1
    A six-pass recursive prompt beat a single pass: 72 against 46 of the 89 points covered on Sonnet, 50 against 38 on Sol, at 2.6 to 4 times the minutes.
  2. Round 4
    A leak check found Codex showing your own installed skills, from the real home folder, to the Sol sessions (7 of 8 in that round). With them switched off, Sol's single pass fell from about 39 to 29, so earlier Sol numbers had been lifted by the leak.
  3. Round 5
    One pass through five "lenses" did as well as iterating, at about half the minutes. And the released stage, run alone, already touched 80 to 87 of the 89 points under both judges. The elaborate prompts mostly turned "partly covered" into "covered".
  4. Round 6
    Adding the lens block to the released stage moved it a little, and not enough. An independent review said not to ship it; checking its claims showed it was right.
  5. Rounds 7 to 10
    One- or two-sentence cues added to the released text, scored only on checklist items sorted by two outside models (figure 2).

Why the outside sort: a coverage count rewards a specification for deciding things, and your rule (27 September) is that product capability the request does not need is overbuilding. So two models that had written none of this sorted the 89 items. They marked 22 as target items about a set or a limit (6 of them limits a correct product cannot do without), and only 4 as capability nobody asked for.

What K's gain was made of

K told the stage to pick a default for any limit the request leaves to choice, and to record it. It did, and some of those limits nobody asked for: 3 of its 4 gym-waitlist specs capped the waitlist (at 10, 10 and 20 people), one added pausing reminders for a single invoice, and one assumed a cutoff 60 minutes before class. The released text had assumed no cap. H allowed a new limit only where the behaviour "cannot work correctly or safely without it", and none of its 4 waitlist specs capped the waitlist.

The lesson from the bench: a count over a specification rewards deciding. It cannot tell rigour from capability nobody asked for, and it cannot tell whether a decision was the right one.

Journal sections 26 and 38 · no model

What 31 real deliveries show

Before concluding, this session read deliveries that had already run on the latest ShipLoop: 26 on Sol and 5 on Sonnet, across five cases. No model was called. Two things stood out.

The same pattern shows elsewhere. The one Sonnet expenses service caps a request at 1 MiB, answers 413 above it, and tests both sides of the line; none of the 10 Sol expenses services has a cap. A Sol chat app had nine limits its builder chose (64 KB requests, 80-character names, 8,000-character messages and more). None is in its spec, knowledge files or README, and no test sends a value that is too long.

Three differences, not one (added 21 November, late morning)

Figure 3 was first read as the bench distorting the stage. A second look found the two differ in three things at once. The bench's requests are 7 to 15 words; the real cases' are 76 to 322. The bench has no repository. And the reference guides, which state what a specification should consider, were read on the bench and are not read in real runs.

Concluded

No template change (07:16, 21 November, at your word). For detailed requests the released stage writes a short, complete specification. Whether it is full enough for a one-sentence request in a real run is not tested. One real difference between the drivers stands: on Sol, a limit nobody stated is chosen silently or not built.

Journal sections 29 to 31 and 37 · results received from the Ratchet session

A text that decides less did better on built products

The Ratchet session measures a specification through what gets built from it. A test suite is graded against a correct reference product, against 37 planted defects, and against held-out checks of the built product. Into its pipeline went a reworded text drafted here, arm R. R tells the writer to mark what the request leaves undecided as an Open: line instead of choosing, and tells the later stages that the build takes a reasonable answer and reports it. Here is a real clause written with it:

C-6: Operators can configure the response hours for each priority without editing code. Observable: Set different hours for urgent, high, normal or low through the service's environment configuration. A ticket newly assigned a due time for that priority uses its configured number of hours. An unset or empty hours setting uses that priority's default. Source: request Open: The requester decides the accepted range and precision for hours, including zero and fractional values. The configuration needs to support changing the stated defaults to usable response durations.

Planted defects caught were level: 36 of 37 for R and for the lens text, 37 of 37 for the pilot's own text. R missed one defect in existing behaviour that its shorter specification no longer pinned. With Sonnet as the writer, R did the same: 3 choice clauses in five specifications against 51, and 42 Open: lines. Sonnet never made the due-times mistake that R repaired on Sol, so that repair is a Sol result.

At medium effort Sonnet wrote the same kind of specification with R (16 to 22 clauses against 21 to 32 at high), and tests built from it caught the same 26 of 27 planted defects, in 5.8 minutes and $1.37 against 14.2 minutes and $2.45. One count from these single runs looked like a price of brevity: five of those 26 catches came only from clauses that run the product's own existing test suite, against none for the lens text's 153 clauses on Sol. Three later runs of that same cell gave 5, 3 and 0, so the count is not shown to follow the specification's length. What stands is the one defect in existing behaviour that the short text's cells miss: anything that adopts it has to say where existing behaviour is pinned.

Journal section 36 · the Ratchet session's fork · this morning

Open in the spec, answered in the plan

If the spec leaves a point open, something still has to pick an answer before anything is built. In these runs the plan does: it lists a default for every open point, and the setting names that go with it. The Ratchet session's fork then laid each run's test suite over the other products, and had two new sessions write tests from the plan alone, with no build present.

The lesson: no test asserted an answer to an open point, but every test still has to start and configure the product, and that is where the answer comes in. So the answers and the names a plan fixes need to be written down before the build, where tests are written from. Reporting them afterwards is too late for anything written separately. The evidence is thin: two sessions, one request, one model, and nobody has yet measured whether tests written this way catch planted defects.

A qualification check, written down first

You asked for a qualification evaluation. The SDLC session had a fix ready, since released as ShipLoop 2.4.1, with three text corrections: run options are the user's to set, and planning reviews default to on; every stage packet now says to stop a background process by the id recorded when it started, never by name; and one guide sentence now sends the spec to the knowledge folder. How we worked: seven lessons

  1. Read deliveries that already ran before building a bench.Section 26 took an hour and no model. It should have been section 1.
  2. Check a bench against the real thing on a plain measure first.Words in the spec would have shown the tenfold gap on day one.
  3. A count that rewards deciding needs its items sorted by someone else.Into what must be settled and what need not, by models that did not write the arms.
  4. Write the prediction down before the run.Eight were registered and six held. The two misses, and one surprise, were the informative results.
  5. Decide after the first round.Dropping the two dead cues after one round would have saved 16 of 96 sessions and 48 of 192 judge calls.
  6. Take the review before the write-up goes anywhere, and check it.Both reviews withdrew claims already made, and both were right on every fact checked.
  7. Judge a wording by what gets built.Three wordings that offered to leave a point open changed nothing in a count over the spec. Through built products, leaving points open did better.

Still open, and yours to decide

  • Do the reference guides make real specifications fuller? On the bench, where sessions read them, the released stage was broad. In real runs they are not opened, and on Sol the reason is plain: the model prints to itself only part of each packet, and the lines that say where the spec guides are reached it in 9 of 50 runs. Now tested in real runs (32 runs from two one-sentence requests, each stopped when its specification was accepted and judged blind): putting two guide sections into the spec packet did not make the specification fuller. On a small user service it covered 38 of 45 item readings against 29 for the released stage, inside that stage's own run-to-run swing; on the richer ticket service it covered 18 against 20. One sentence telling the model to read its whole packet made it read the reference list and open the requirements guide, and the specification was no fuller. What the released stage leaves thin is specific and the same in every arm: on the ticket service, the per-row result, what the file may set, the flood of notifications, an audit of the import, hostile content, and dates. The gap is particular questions nobody asks, not guidance on how to write requirements. A third test then made the spec stage fill a section answering ShipLoop's own twelve key questions: the section was written every time, twelve entries, and it read as an index of what the requirements already said. Coverage did not move (21 of 27 against 18, and 21 of 45 against 20). A fourth test asked five particular questions, drawn from those thin items, as a section to fill, on two fresh requests (merge duplicate tickets; remind requesters who keep a ticket waiting) whose checklists were written by sessions that had seen none of this. The section was written every time and was again an index; coverage did not move (34 of 63 against 36, and 14 of 38 against 15), and the questions did not reliably raise even the items they name. Four levers tried, none shown. On the blind checklists the released stage addresses nearly every item (60 of 63 and 36 of 38 at least in part) and fully settles about half; what is short is how completely each many-part item is settled, and asking does not move it. One run with the questions stopped and asked you how a ticket comes to be waiting, where the other five decided it themselves. The packet's wording no longer looks like the lever. A fifth test changed the author instead: the same unchanged stage on Claude Sonnet beside GPT Sol, both at medium, three pairs on each of the two fresh requests. By the rule set beforehand it is not shown: items fully covered under both judges were 35 of 63 for each on the merge request, and 25 of 57 for Sonnet against 19 for Sol on the reminders request. As a description: Sonnet writes about two thirds more (985 words against 595) in the same three minutes, and on Sonnet the stage's review changes the specification every time (9% to 43% more words) where Sol's changes nothing in five of six. On the reminders request Sonnet was ahead in all nine pairings and kept to the program's existing resolved state, where every Sol specification added a new ticket status. On the merge request the two were level, and every Sonnet specification added a notification nobody asked for. Neither author is the careful one, and none of these specifications was built. Which driver to use is a question about whole deliveries, which the SDLC session reads.
  • Try "open in the spec, answered in the plan" in ShipLoop? Nothing is proposed yet. It would need your word, a candidate build and real end-to-end deliveries, and it is untested where one session writes the plan, the tests and the product in turn.

Words used on this page

Spec stage
The step of a ShipLoop run that turns the request into numbered requirements, each with lines saying how it will be checked.
Prompt text, or arm
One version of the instructions given to that stage. P is the released text; letters such as K, G, H and R are variants.
Driver
The model that runs the stage: Claude Sonnet, or GPT 6.2 Sol (through Codex).
Hidden checklist
The 89 points a good specification should cover, written before any run and never shown to the models.
Judge
A model that marks which checklist points a specification covers. Opus was registered first; Astra was the second judge.
Band
The smallest difference counted as real, set from how much two runs of the same text differ.
Open point
Something the request leaves undecided. With the reworded text it is written as an Open: line, not chosen.
Held-out check
A test of the built product written beforehand, which the run never sees.
Correct reference
A hand-built product that does what was asked, used to see whether a test suite wrongly fails a correct product.
Qualification
Evidence that a ShipLoop change still delivers whole projects end to end.
